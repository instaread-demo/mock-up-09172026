MoN Takanawa: The Museum of Narratives Is Experientially Compelling But Curatorially Thin

Kengo Kuma’s newly opened institution offers a striking architectural vision of the museum of the future, even as its inaugural exhibition struggles to present a coherent cultural narrative.

MoN Takanawa: The Museum of Narratives recently opened in Tokyo’s new Takanawa Gateway City commercial and business complex. Courtesy MoN Takanawa

The highly anticipated Kengo Kuma-designed MoN Takanawa opened this past spring, conceived as a model for the museum of the future. Its stated mission is to transport culture for the next hundred years — translating the layering of disciplines that shape our reality and imagination, from art and science to technology, urbanism and mythology, into a single continuous storyline through its fluid, spiralling architecture.

The building sits at the heart of Takanawa Gateway City, the new commercial and business complex rising around one of Tokyo’s newest rail stations. Kuma’s design answers that setting with stacked, offset rings of timber louvres and planting, so the volume reads as a spiral of horizontal bands rather than a conventional box — a form that looks different from every approach.

Inside, the spiral becomes the visitor route. Rather than a sequence of discrete galleries, the museum unfolds as a continuous ascent, with sightlines that keep the city visible alongside the work on display. It is a genuinely persuasive piece of spatial storytelling, and the strongest argument the institution makes for its own thesis.

The inaugural exhibition is the weaker half of the proposition. Individual commissions are absorbing — kinetic and computational works that reward time spent with them — but the curatorial frame gathers them under a theme broad enough to accommodate almost anything, and the promised through-line between disciplines never quite materialises as argument.

The result is a museum that is experientially compelling and curatorially thin: a building that tells a story with real conviction, housing a first show still working out what that story is. On the evidence of the architecture alone, it is worth the trip — and worth returning to once the programme catches up.
